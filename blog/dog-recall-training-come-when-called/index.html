Dog recall training: teach your dog to come when called

A reliable recall can save your dog's life. It's also one of the easiest skills to wreck. Here's how to build it properly.

By Alex Morgan, CPDT-KA2026-11-128 min read

Few skills matter more than a solid recall. It's the one that keeps your dog out of traffic, away from a snake on the trail, or simply lets them enjoy off-leash time. It's also the cue most dogs aren't good at, because owners accidentally train them to ignore it.

Why dogs don't come

  • It's boring. The squirrel is better than you.
  • It ends the fun. If "come" always means leash on and walk over, why would they hurry?
  • It's unpredictable. Sometimes it's a treat, sometimes a telling-off.
  • They've never practised with distractions. A cue that works in the kitchen doesn't work at Zilker.

Rules of a good recall

  1. Never call your dog to scold them. Ever.
  2. Reward every correct recall. Early on, with something truly delicious.
  3. Use one word, once. Say "come" cheerfully. Not "come, come, COME!"
  4. Practise often, in short bursts.
  5. Don't ask for recall in situations you can't enforce.

Step 1: Start inside

Stand a few steps away. Say your dog's name and "come," then reward the moment they arrive. Add a little game: back away to make it exciting. Repeat in different rooms.

Step 2: Take it outside on a long line

Use a 15 to 30 foot line in a safe area. Let your dog wander, call them, and reward generously. If they don't respond, gently guide them in with the line. Don't yank. The line is for safety, not correction.

Step 3: Build distractions slowly

Practise where there's a little more going on, like a quiet park, then a busier one. Rate the difficulty and match your reward. A recall past a leaf deserves a good treat. A recall past a squirrel deserves a jackpot of five.

Step 4: Make coming back the start of fun

Call your dog, reward them, and then release them to play again. This teaches them that coming to you doesn't end the party. Mix it up. Sometimes clip the lead on, treat, and then let them go again.

Step 5: Add an emergency cue

Pick a second word (like "here!") reserved for danger, always paired with the best reward your dog gets. Practise it a few times a week so it stays powerful.

Mistakes that undo progress

  • Chasing your dog. They'll think it's a game and run away.
  • Letting your dog off leash too early. Every failed recall rehearses ignoring you.
  • Calling when you're angry or frustrated. Your tone matters.
  • Only calling to end the walk.

Is off-leash training right for every dog?

Not always. Dogs with strong prey drive, reactivity or very little recall history may never be fully off-leash safe in open spaces, and that's fine. A long line gives them freedom with safety. If you want to build real off-leash reliability, a trainer can plan the stages with you. Our obedience sessions include recall and impulse-control work.

Safety note: in Texas, check local leash laws and trail rules before any off-leash work. Use fenced areas for practice.

Quick answers

How long does it take to train a reliable recall?

Usually several months of regular practice, with distractions added gradually. Many dogs are reliable in low distraction settings within a few weeks.

Why does my dog ignore me outside?

Outdoors has far more exciting competition than home. The recall hasn't been practised with enough distractions or rewards.

Should I use a long line?

Yes, in safe areas. It lets your dog explore while you keep control and prevents practising ignoring the cue.

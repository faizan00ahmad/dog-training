How to choose a dog trainer in Austin: 9 questions to ask first

Anyone can call themselves a dog trainer. Here's how to separate the qualified, kind ones from the rest, before your dog pays the price.

By Alex Morgan, CPDT-KA2026-11-177 min read

In most places, including Texas, dog training isn't regulated. There's no licence you need to hang a sign and call yourself a trainer. That means quality varies enormously, and the cheapest option isn't always the safest.

Here are nine questions to ask before you book. A good trainer will be glad you asked.

1. What methods and equipment do you use?

You want a clear, plain answer. Look for reward-based, force-free training. If a trainer relies on prong, choke or shock collars, or talks about "dominance" and "being the alpha," look elsewhere. Those ideas have been rejected by modern behaviour science.

2. What qualifications do you hold?

Certifications like CPDT-KA, KPA-CTP or IAABC show a trainer has been assessed and follows an ethics code. They aren't the whole story, but they're a good sign. Ask what continuing education they do.

3. Are you insured?

Especially for in-home visits. A professional carries liability insurance and is happy to confirm it.

4. How do you handle a dog who's scared or overwhelmed?

The right answer involves slowing down and giving the dog space. If the answer is "we push through," that's a red flag.

5. Will I be part of the training?

You should be. A trainer who works with your dog alone and hands back a "fixed" dog isn't teaching you how to keep it fixed. Good training coaches the humans too.

6. What does the first session look like?

Expect a conversation about your dog, observation, and a written or clear plan. If you're told what to do within five minutes without a single question, be wary.

7. What are your prices and what's included?

You should know session length, travel fees, package options and what support is included between sessions. See our Austin dog training price guide for typical ranges.

8. Can I see reviews or talk to past clients?

Look for detailed reviews that describe a specific problem and what changed. Generic five-star comments say little. Check Google, Yelp and independent sources.

9. What do you do if my dog doesn't improve?

Good trainers don't promise miracles. They explain what's realistic, adjust the plan, and tell you when a case needs a vet or a behaviourist.

Red flags to walk away from

  • Guaranteed results in one week.
  • "Alpha," "dominance" or "balanced" used to justify corrections.
  • No insurance, no references.
  • Refusing to explain the methods.
  • Board-and-train with no visits, updates or video.
  • Pressure to buy the biggest package at the first meeting.

What good looks like

A calm, curious trainer who watches your dog, asks you plenty of questions, and explains what they'll do and why. You should leave feeling clearer, not scolded. Dogs relax around people like that too.

One more tip: ask to watch a session or see a video. Calm dogs and relaxed owners say more than any website.

Quick answers

What certifications should a dog trainer have?

Common credible ones include CPDT-KA, KPA-CTP and IAABC credentials, along with a commitment to force-free, humane methods.

Is dog training regulated in Texas?

No. Anyone can offer dog training, so checking methods, qualifications and insurance matters.

What are red flags when hiring a dog trainer?

Guaranteed overnight results, reliance on shock or prong collars, dominance talk, no insurance, and refusing to explain the methods.

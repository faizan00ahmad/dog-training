Separation anxiety or boredom: how to tell the difference

Chewed doors and neighbour complaints don't always mean what you think. Here's how to work out what's really happening when you leave.

By Alex Morgan, CPDT-KA2026-11-148 min read

You come home to a chewed sofa, a shredded cushion or a note from the neighbour about barking. It's easy to assume your dog is angry or spiteful. They aren't. They're struggling with something, and the question is what.

Separation anxiety: the signs

True separation anxiety is panic about being alone. Typical signs include:

  • Distress that begins as you get ready to leave: pacing, panting, following you from room to room.
  • Howling, barking or whining that continues for long stretches.
  • Destruction focused on exits: doors, windowsills, crate bars.
  • Toileting indoors even though they're house trained.
  • Drooling, shaking, or refusing food while you're away.

Boredom: the signs

A bored dog is annoyed rather than terrified. You'll see:

  • Chewing or digging at random objects, not the exits.
  • No distress as you leave. The dog is calm or even eager to see you go.
  • Destruction in the first hour, then sleep.
  • A dog who calms down quickly with exercise or enrichment.

A simple test is to record your dog on your phone for the first hour you're out. What you see tells you a lot. A dog settling with a chew toy is not the same as a dog scratching at the door.

What else could it be?

  • Medical problems. Pain, digestive trouble or age-related changes can look like anxiety. Check with your vet.
  • Noise phobia. Thunder, fireworks or construction can cause panic when you're away.
  • Inadequate exercise. Some dogs need far more than a short stroll.

What helps with boredom

  • A proper walk before you leave, with sniffing time.
  • Food puzzles, frozen stuffed toys, lick mats.
  • Rotating toys so they stay interesting.
  • A dog walker or day care for long days.

What helps with separation anxiety

This needs a gentler, slower approach. The goal is to teach your dog that being alone is safe, one small step at a time.

  1. Find the "safe" duration. If your dog is calm for 30 seconds, start there.
  2. Practise short absences. Leave for a few seconds, return before they get upset, and slowly extend.
  3. Keep departures and arrivals calm. No big dramatic goodbyes.
  4. Use cues that signal you'll be back. A treat toy only given when you leave can build positive associations.
  5. Don't leave a panicking dog alone for long. Arrange cover while you train. Each panic episode sets progress back.

What not to do

  • Don't punish. Your dog won't connect it with the mess and it will deepen the fear.
  • Don't rely on crates for dogs who panic in them.
  • Don't wait. Anxiety tends to get worse without help.

When to ask for help

If you suspect real anxiety, bring in a trainer with experience, and talk to your vet about whether medication may help alongside training. Our behaviour programme starts with a detailed assessment in your home so we can see where and when it happens.

Good to know: a lot of dogs who adjusted to lockdown routines struggled when owners returned to the office. If you recently changed schedules, that may be the trigger.

Quick answers

How do I know if my dog has separation anxiety?

Distress that starts when you prepare to leave, continuous barking or howling, exit-focused destruction and indoor accidents are common signs. A video recording helps tell it apart from boredom.

Can separation anxiety be cured?

Many dogs improve a lot with gradual training, and some need support from a vet as well. Progress should be slow and consistent.

Will a second dog help?

Not usually. Many anxious dogs are attached to a person, not just company.

Leash reactive dog: what's really going on and what helps

A dog who explodes at other dogs usually isn't aggressive. They're stressed. Understanding why is the first step to a quieter walk.

By Alex Morgan, CPDT-KA2026-10-279 min read

If you've ever crossed the street to avoid another dog, apologised to a stranger while your dog screamed, or started walking at 5am just to stay out of trouble, you already know how isolating leash reactivity feels. First, the good news: it's common, and it's very workable.

What "reactive" actually means

A reactive dog overreacts to something specific, most often other dogs, bikes, skateboards, strangers or cars. The reaction can look aggressive: barking, lunging, growling, spinning. Most of the time it isn't. It's a dog saying "this is too much, make it go away."

The common causes are:

  • Fear. The dog feels trapped on a leash and can't run away, so they act big to make the scary thing leave.
  • Frustration. The dog wants to say hello but can't get there. The pent-up excitement boils over.
  • Lack of early socialisation. Pups who missed positive experiences in their first months often struggle later.
  • A bad experience. One scuffle at a dog park can shape a dog's feelings for years.

The trouble is that yelling or yanking the leash tends to make the dog link the other dog with something unpleasant, which makes it worse. That's why we avoid corrections.

Step 1: Find your dog's threshold

Every reactive dog has a distance at which they can still notice another dog without exploding. That's the "threshold." Start there. If your dog reacts at 50 feet, you're too close. Back up until they stay relaxed, take a treat and look at you.

Step 2: Change what the other dog means

At a distance where your dog is calm, say "yes" the moment they spot the other dog and give them a treat. You're teaching them that dogs appearing predicts good things. Over several weeks you can slowly reduce the distance, always staying below the point where your dog loses control.

Step 3: Give your dog an exit

A lot of reactivity is caused by a dog feeling cornered. Learn to leave. A simple "let's go" cue and a calm U-turn teaches your dog that you've got them. It also builds trust, which is half of the work.

Step 4: Manage the rest of the day

Dogs who are stressed at home are more reactive outside. Make sure they're getting enough sleep, exercise and enrichment. Sniffing walks, puzzle feeders and quiet time all lower the overall stress load. If it's been a bad week, give your dog a break from the trigger completely.

What not to do

  • Don't punish growling. It's a warning, and removing it can make a dog bite without notice.
  • Don't force "meet and greets" to cure it.
  • Don't use prong or shock collars. They can suppress the signs while increasing the fear underneath.
  • Don't give up. Reactivity improves in most dogs with consistent, calm work.

When to get help

If your dog has bitten, if you can't read when they're about to react, or if you're simply exhausted, a trainer can speed things up and keep everyone safe. In-home sessions are especially useful, since we can start with triggers you can control, like dogs passing the window. Our behaviour training programme starts with an assessment so we know exactly what's driving the reaction.

A note on safety: if your dog has bitten or you're worried they might, speak to your vet first to rule out pain and then to a qualified trainer. Please don't try to fix it alone.

Quick answers

Can a leash reactive dog be cured?

Many reactive dogs improve dramatically with consistent, reward-based training, though some will always need a bit of management. Progress is the goal, and it's very achievable.

Should I let my reactive dog meet other dogs?

Not as a cure. Forced meetings often make things worse. Build calm distance work first.

Why does my dog only react on the leash?

A leash removes your dog's ability to move away or greet normally, which can create fear or frustration that doesn't appear off leash.
